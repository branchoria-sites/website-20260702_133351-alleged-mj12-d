## Introduction

By the time *The X-Files* premiered in 1993, allegations about secret UFO programmes and documents such as the purported MJ-12 papers were already circulating within UFO [research circles]({{'research-circle/' | relative_url }}). The television series did not introduce these ideas, but it transformed them into mainstream entertainment. Instead of requiring audiences to read specialist books or newsletters, it presented hidden files, compartmentalised government programmes and official denial as recurring dramatic themes watched by millions every week. In doing so, the programme helped make the logic of MJ-12-style suspicion familiar even to viewers who had never heard of the alleged documents themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://shura.shu.ac.uk/31823/9/Clarke-WantToBelieve%28AM%29.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shura.shu.ac.uk">[SHURA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shura.shu.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SHURAI want to believe: how UFOs conquered the X-files</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">May 3, 2023 — by D Clarke · 2023 — As the episode unfolds, elements of this and other contemporary UFO legends are name-checked: missing...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 3, 2023</span></span></span><img src="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_ufo_culture_impact_b3cafb_xfiles_mj12_suspicio_04d5ea-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="X Files Mood illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_ufo_culture_impact_b3cafb_xfiles_mj12_suspicio_04d5ea-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_ufo_culture_impact_b3cafb_xfiles_mj12_suspicio_04d5ea-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">
The result was a cultural shift. During the 1990s, the question was no longer confined to whether UFOs existed, but whether governments routinely concealed extraordinary information from the public. That broader suspicion became one of *The X-Files*' defining legacies and provided fertile ground for continued interest in alleged MJ-12 material, regardless of its disputed [authenticity]({{'authenticity/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0392192120924536" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.sagepub.com">[Sage Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.sagepub.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sage JournalsConspiracy theories and UFOs - Damien Karbovnik, 201515 Oct 2020 — In exchange for this technology, mj 12 guaranteed the sec...</span></span></span>

## Files as a Popular Symbol of Truth

One reason the alleged MJ-12 papers proved so influential was that they treated paperwork itself as evidence. The attraction lay not only in claims about recovered alien technology but in the appearance of official memoranda, briefing documents and [classified markings]({{'secret-marks/' | relative_url }}). *The X-Files* adopted the same visual language. Government [archives]({{'archives/' | relative_url }}), sealed folders, redacted reports and forgotten files became recurring symbols of hidden truth waiting to be uncovered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://shura.shu.ac.uk/31823/9/Clarke-WantToBelieve%28AM%29.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shura.shu.ac.uk">[SHURA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shura.shu.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SHURAI want to believe: how UFOs conquered the X-files</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">May 3, 2023 — by D Clarke · 2023 — As the episode unfolds, elements of this and other contemporary UFO legends are name-checked: missing...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 3, 2023</span></span></span>

The series encouraged audiences to think about knowledge in bureaucratic terms. Truth was imagined as something preserved inside filing cabinets, intelligence vaults and classified databases rather than simply witnessed in the field. This closely echoed the appeal of [the alleged MJ-12 documents]({{'the-alleged-mj-12-documents/' | relative_url }}), which derived much of their persuasive power from looking like leaked government records rather than eyewitness testimony alone.

## Government Denial on Screen

Rather than presenting government secrecy as a single conspiracy, *The X-Files* depicted a fragmented network of military officers, intelligence officials, scientists and political insiders operating through compartmentalised authority. That structure closely resembled the narrative function served by MJ-12 in UFO literature: a hidden administrative layer above ordinary government institutions.

Importantly, the series rarely provided complete answers. Evidence disappeared, witnesses died, files were destroyed and official explanations remained deliberately ambiguous. This storytelling technique mirrored an important feature of conspiracy narratives: incomplete evidence became part of the mystery instead of resolving it. Viewers were encouraged to interpret missing information as consistent with concealment rather than simply with uncertainty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://shura.shu.ac.uk/31780/3/Fenwick-IntroductionACriticalReflectionOnThirtyYearsOfTheXFiles%28AM%29.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shura.shu.ac.uk">[SHURA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shura.shu.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Introduction: a critical reflection on thirty years of The X- Filesby J Fenwick · 2023 — ” The episodes that follows these injunctio...</span></span></span>

The programme also normalised the idea that multiple agencies might pursue conflicting agendas while sharing an overarching commitment to secrecy. That portrayal was more sophisticated than a simple"government lies" storyline and reflected the increasingly elaborate cover-up narratives that had developed around Roswell, Area 51 and the alleged MJ-12 papers during the 1980s.<img src="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_ufo_culture_impact_b3cafb_xfiles_mj12_suspicio_04d5ea-Illustration-2-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="X Files Mood illustration 2" data-theme-src-dark="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_ufo_culture_impact_b3cafb_xfiles_mj12_suspicio_04d5ea-Illustration-2-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_ufo_culture_impact_b3cafb_xfiles_mj12_suspicio_04d5ea-Illustration-2-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">
## From UFO Subculture to Shared Suspicion

Before the 1990s, detailed discussions of MJ-12 generally remained within specialist UFO magazines, conferences and books. *The X-Files* acted as a bridge between that subculture and mainstream popular culture.

Millions of viewers who never read UFO literature nevertheless became familiar with recurring ideas that paralleled MJ-12 claims:<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-stack" markdown="1">

* hidden committees directing secret programmes;
* recovered non-human technology kept from the public;
* classified files containing forbidden knowledge;
* intelligence agencies suppressing witnesses;
* whistle-blowers facing intimidation or disappearance.</div>

The programme seldom depended on the alleged MJ-12 documents themselves. Instead, it popularised the broader narrative framework into which those documents fit naturally. As a result, audiences could later encounter references to"Majestic-12" or similar alleged organisations in books, documentaries or online discussions without needing a detailed introduction to the underlying mythology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://shura.shu.ac.uk/31823/9/Clarke-WantToBelieve%28AM%29.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shura.shu.ac.uk">[SHURA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shura.shu.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SHURAI want to believe: how UFOs conquered the X-files</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">May 3, 2023 — by D Clarke · 2023 — As the episode unfolds, elements of this and other contemporary UFO legends are name-checked: missing...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 3, 2023</span></span></span>

This shift also changed public expectations. UFO stories increasingly came to involve [bureaucracy]({{'bureaucracy/' | relative_url }}), classified projects and institutional secrecy rather than isolated sightings. In popular imagination, the cover-up often became more compelling than the unidentified object.

## Why the Show Strengthened MJ-12's Cultural Presence

The cultural importance of *The X-Files* did not depend on validating the alleged MJ-12 documents. Official investigations reached the opposite conclusion. The FBI's publicly released files describe the key MJ-12 document received by investigators as"completely bogus", while archival reviews found no evidence that the purported executive documents originated within the United States government.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/Majestic%2012" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic 12</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Majestic 12Majestic 12 In 1988, two FBI offices received similar versions of a memo titled “Operation Majestic-12…” claiming to be hig...</span></span></span>

Yet entertainment operates differently from historical authentication. Because *The X-Files* presented hidden government programmes as plausible dramatic material week after week, it helped sustain public interest in ideas that resembled the MJ-12 narrative even after official criticism of the documents became widely available. The fictional world did not prove the documents genuine, but it made their underlying assumptions feel culturally familiar.

This distinction is important. The series contributed to the mainstreaming of suspicion rather than the acceptance of any specific document. It demonstrated how a fictional narrative could amplify the cultural reach of contested claims by making their basic structure—secret files, compartmentalised authority and official denial—appear both recognisable and dramatically believable.<img src="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_ufo_culture_impact_b3cafb_xfiles_mj12_suspicio_04d5ea-Illustration-3-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="X Files Mood illustration 3" data-theme-src-dark="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_ufo_culture_impact_b3cafb_xfiles_mj12_suspicio_04d5ea-Illustration-3-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_ufo_culture_impact_b3cafb_xfiles_mj12_suspicio_04d5ea-Illustration-3-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">
## A Lasting Cultural Legacy

The enduring influence of *The X-Files* lies less in its treatment of individual UFO cases than in its reshaping of public expectations about secrecy. It helped establish the image of hidden government archives as a central symbol of unexplained phenomena and turned the search for concealed documents into a familiar feature of popular culture.
