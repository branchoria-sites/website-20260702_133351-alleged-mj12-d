## Introduction

The alleged Truman authorisation memo is the hinge of the MJ-12 story. It is the document that turns a rumour about recovered UFO debris into a claim of presidentially approved machinery: Harry S. Truman supposedly told Secretary of Defence [James Forrestal]({{'forrestal/' | relative_url }}), on 24 September 1947, to proceed with “Operation Majestic Twelve” after consultation with [Vannevar Bush]({{'bush/' | relative_url }}). The problem is not just that the memo is disputed. It is that a genuine presidential authorisation of this kind should have left traces in executive orders, White House files, security-control records, agency indexes and later administrative correspondence. The strongest official record does not show that trail. The National Archives reports extensive negative searches for MJ-12 material, GAO found no evidence that the material originated as executive-branch documents, and the FBI’s file preserves the government view that the submitted MJ-12 papers were not genuine government records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives+2GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span></span></span><img src="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_truman_authorisation_aafe47-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Truman Memo" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">
## What the Alleged Memo Claims

The Truman-Forrestal memo is usually treated as “Attachment A” to the wider alleged Eisenhower briefing document. In the MJ-12 narrative, the briefing says that Operation Majestic-12 was a [top-secret]({{'top-secret/' | relative_url }}) research, development and intelligence operation answering “directly and only” to the President. It claims the group was created by a “special classified executive order” of President Truman on 24 September 1947, on the recommendation of Vannevar Bush and James Forrestal. The reproduced text then lists twelve senior military, scientific and intelligence figures as [members]({{'members/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/MJ-12_Document" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MJ-12 Document</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MJ-12 Document</span></span></span>

The separate alleged Truman memo is brief and dramatic. It presents Truman as authorising Forrestal to proceed “with all due speed and caution”, gives the matter the name “Operation Majestic Twelve”, and says future decisions should rest with the Office of the President after discussion with Forrestal, Bush and the Director of Central Intelligence. In storytelling terms, that is why this page of the MJ-12 papers matters so much: it supplies a presidential [signature]({{'signature/' | relative_url }}), a date, a named chain of advice and a reason why later investigators would be looking not just for UFO files, but for a hidden presidentially controlled programme.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://majesticdocuments.com/pdf/truman_forrestal.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: majesticdocuments.com">[majesticdocuments.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">majesticdocuments.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">President Truman to Secretary of Defense James Forrestal</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">President Truman to Secretary of Defense James Forrestal</span></span></span>

There is one real-world fact that gives the alleged date an initial air of [plausibility]({{'plausibility/' | relative_url }}). Truman’s appointment calendar for 24 September 1947 lists an 11:30 a.m. meeting with Secretary of Defence James Forrestal and Dr Vannevar Bush, arranged at Forrestal’s suggestion. That does not authenticate the memo; it merely shows that two named people in the alleged authorisation were indeed scheduled to see Truman that day. For a forger, a real appointment could provide useful camouflage. For a believer, it is an important coincidence. For an archivist, it is only a starting point, because a meeting is not the same thing as a signed classified presidential directive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trumanlibrary.gov/calendar/search?body_value_op=word&amp;comment_keywords=forrestal&amp;page=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trumanlibrary.gov">[Truman Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trumanlibrary.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Truman Library Daily Appointments of Harry S. ## Why Presidential Authority Matters

The MJ-12 claim needs Truman because the alleged programme is not just a scientific committee or a military recovery team. It is presented as a secret structure sitting above ordinary agencies, able to handle recovered technology, bodies, intelligence, public disclosure planning and presidential briefings. Without presidential authorisation, the alleged group would be much harder to square with the developing post-war [national security]({{'1947-context/' | relative_url }}) system. With Truman’s signature attached, the documents invite the reader to believe that extraordinary secrecy was lawful because it came from the top.

That timing matters. The National Security Act of 1947 had just reorganised the United States’ military and intelligence institutions. It created the National Security Council and the Central Intelligence Agency, and placed the new Secretary of Defence inside a formal national security structure. The Office of the Historian describes the Act as a major reorganisation of the foreign policy and military establishments, while the Act’s own language speaks of integrated policies, civilian control through the Secretary of Defence, unified strategic direction and clearer command arrangements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://history.state.gov/milestones/1945-1952/national-security-act" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.state.gov">[Office of the Historian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.state.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Office of the Historian Milestones in the History of U.S. Foreign Relations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Office of the Historian Milestones in the History of U.S. Foreign Relations</span></span></span>

This creates a tension for the alleged Truman memo. On one hand, 24 September 1947 was a believable moment for sensitive national security arrangements: Forrestal was newly in post, Bush had wartime scientific prestige, and the CIA and NSC were just being embedded. On the other hand, a genuine presidentially approved special project sitting “directly and only” under the President would normally be expected to intersect with at least some records of that new machinery. Even if the operational details were compartmented, the authorising instrument, security classification status, distribution controls, file cross-references or later custody trail should have been visible somewhere in the systems created to manage classified national security work.

That is why the authority claim is more testable than a general UFO rumour. The question is not whether a president could ever authorise a classified operation. Presidents plainly did authorise secret national security activity. The question is whether this specific alleged authorisation behaves like a real Truman-era presidential record when checked against the archival systems around Truman, Forrestal, Bush, the NSC, the Air Force, the Joint Chiefs, classification oversight and later executive-branch custody.<img src="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_truman_authorisation_aafe47-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Truman Memo illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_truman_authorisation_aafe47-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_truman_authorisation_aafe47-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">
## What a Genuine Record Trail Should Show

A genuine Truman authorisation of MJ-12 would not have to be public in 1947 to leave evidence. Classified records are still records. They are created, routed, indexed, controlled, copied, retired, reviewed and sometimes withheld with withdrawal sheets or classification markings. A document could remain secret, but its existence would normally have institutional shadows.

The expected trail would include several kinds of evidence:

* **A presidential document trail.** If the claim is a “special classified executive order”, there should be some credible relationship to the numbered executive-order system or to a defensible category of classified presidential directives. The Truman Library describes executive orders as official documents, numbered consecutively, used by presidents to manage federal operations. The public lists do not prove that no classified directive could ever exist, but the alleged “Order #092447” looks more like a date turned into a pseudo-number than a normal executive-order identifier.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trumanlibrary.gov/library/executive-orders?page=24" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trumanlibrary.gov">[Truman Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trumanlibrary.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on trumanlibrary.gov.</span></span></span>
* **White House or library references.** A signed instruction to Forrestal involving Bush and the Director of Central Intelligence should have had a plausible location in Truman Library holdings, White House central files, Forrestal-related correspondence, Bush files or national security records. GAO reported that archivists at the Truman and Eisenhower libraries searched related records, including classified intelligence and National Security Council documents, and found nothing fitting the MJ-12 description or referring to that designation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/154832.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Comments on Majestic 12 Material</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Comments on Majestic 12 Material</span></span></span>
* **Agency custody or classification evidence.** If the memo was truly an executive-branch classified record, some agency should be able to recognise its origin, control system or classification handling. GAO said the Information Security Oversight Office, the Air Force and the National Archives knew of the material only from submissions by non-governmental persons, found no related records in their files, and agreed there was no evidence that the written material constituted documents originally created in the executive branch.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/154832.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Comments on Majestic 12 Material</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Comments on Majestic 12 Material</span></span></span>
* **Follow-on administrative records.** A presidentially authorised standing group would normally generate later paper: meeting notices, budget channels, clearance rosters, memoranda, status reports, personnel replacements, records-control decisions or declassification traces. The National Archives says it searched Air Force and Joint Chiefs records, and that the Truman and Eisenhower Libraries searched their holdings for references or copies of the documents. The reported result was negative apart from the separately disputed Cutler-Twining item, which is outside the Truman memo itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span></span></span>

This absence is not a small technicality. It is the central evidentiary problem. The Truman memo claims to be the founding authority for an organisation of immense sensitivity. ## The Signature Problem

The most damaging document-specific challenge concerns Truman’s signature. Philip J. Klass, one of the most prominent sceptical investigators of the MJ-12 papers, argued that the signature on the Truman-Forrestal memo was a pasted-on photocopy taken from a genuine Truman memo to Vannevar Bush dated 1 October 1947, including accidental scratch marks. In his later summary of the case, Klass described the Truman memo as one of the original MJ-12 documents released in 1987 and said the alleged Eisenhower briefing document’s dependence on that memo was one of its own major weaknesses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://centerforinquiry.s3.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2000/05/22164853/p44.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: centerforinquiry.s3.amazonaws.com">[centerforinquiry.s3.amazonaws.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">centerforinquiry.s3.amazonaws.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on amazonaws.com.</span></span></span>

That claim matters because the signature is doing heavy evidentiary work. The alleged memo is short; its power lies in the White House heading, the date, the addressee and Truman’s name. If the signature was mechanically reproduced from an unrelated genuine document, the memo would no longer be a leaked presidential authorisation. It would be a constructed artefact borrowing a real presidential signature to simulate authority.

Supporters of the MJ-12 documents have disputed the signature argument. Stanton Friedman, whose defence of the original MJ-12 papers remains one of the best-known pro-[authenticity]({{'authenticity/' | relative_url }}) positions, argued that the memo should not be dismissed simply because the signature appears reproduced. He cited later document-examination claims about typewriter compatibility, noted that some presidential documents were prepared by others, and suggested that copies might plausibly contain a lifted or reproduced signature rather than an original wet-ink signature. The Black Vault’s republication of Friedman’s argument also notes a concession from a document examiner that the disputed signature was “most likely a reproduction”, while framing that as not necessarily fatal to authenticity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/casefiles/update-on-operation-majestic-12-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[theblackvault.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Update on Operation Majestic 12 Documents</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Update on Operation Majestic 12 Documents</span></span></span>

The difficulty for the pro-authenticity position is that this defence turns the memo into a less direct kind of evidence. If the signature is not an original signature on the surviving copy, then authentication depends even more heavily on provenance and record trail. But those are precisely the areas where the official archival record is weakest for MJ-12.<img src="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_truman_authorisation_aafe47-Illustration-2-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Truman Memo illustration 2" data-theme-src-dark="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_truman_authorisation_aafe47-Illustration-2-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_truman_authorisation_aafe47-Illustration-2-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">
## The Real Meeting Does Not Carry the Memo

The 24 September 1947 calendar entry is the strongest narrow fact in favour of taking the date seriously. Truman did have Forrestal and Bush on the schedule that morning. A reader should not ignore that. It is a concrete archival anchor, and it explains why the alleged memo feels better fitted to history than many crude UFO hoaxes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trumanlibrary.gov/calendar/search?body_value_op=word&amp;comment_keywords=forrestal&amp;page=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trumanlibrary.gov">[Truman Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trumanlibrary.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Truman Library Daily Appointments of Harry S. But the calendar entry cannot bear the weight placed on it. The appointment line does not mention Roswell, UFOs, “Majestic”, an executive order, the Director of Central Intelligence, recovered material, a special studies group or any classified project. It is also surrounded by the ordinary churn of the new national security state: two days later, Truman’s calendar lists a National Security Council meeting with Forrestal, Robert Lovett, Arthur Hill, the service secretaries, Sidney Souers and Roscoe Hillenkoetter. That context makes a national security meeting unsurprising, not proof of MJ-12.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trumanlibrary.gov/calendar/search?body_value_op=word&amp;comment_keywords=forrestal&amp;page=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trumanlibrary.gov">[Truman Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trumanlibrary.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Truman Library Daily Appointments of Harry S. This distinction is crucial. A genuine historical coincidence can make a forged document more persuasive. The question is not whether the alleged memo cleverly aligns with a real appointment. The question is whether independent records turn that appointment into the claimed authorisation. So far, official searches have not done so.

## The Official Findings Weigh Against the Authority Claim

The National Archives’ position is especially important because the Truman authorisation claim is an archival question before it is a UFO question. The Archives says it received many inquiries about “MJ12” and the “Briefing Document: Operation Majestic 12”, then made extensive searches in Air Force and Joint Chiefs records; the Truman and Eisenhower Libraries also searched for references or copies. That is exactly the sort of inquiry the alleged founding memo invites.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span></span></span>

## Why the Memo Remains Persuasive to Some Readers

The Truman memo’s appeal is not hard to understand. It is short, confident and placed at a historically plausible moment. It names powerful people who really existed. Forrestal had just become Secretary of Defence after the National Security Act took effect; Bush had been central to wartime science; the CIA and NSC were new; the early Cold War was full of real secrecy. The alleged memo therefore fits a mood of 1947 even where it fails as a documented presidential record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/briefing-book/intelligence/2022-07-26/national-security-act-turns-75" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive.gwu.edu">[National Security Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">national security act turns 75</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">national security act turns 75</span></span></span>

Supporters also point to the fact that not all secret programmes produce easy public trails, and that absence of a found record is not mathematically the same as proof of non-existence. That caution is reasonable in the abstract. Archives can be incomplete, records can be misfiled, and classified systems can be opaque. But the MJ-12 claim is not a minor missing memo. It is a claim that Truman created a long-running, president-only control structure for an extraordinary subject. The greater the claimed authority, the more damaging the absence of ordinary record behaviour becomes.

The best balanced reading is therefore not that the Truman meeting proves MJ-12, nor that a single anomaly alone settles every question. It is that the alleged authorisation memo has one historically interesting anchor, but lacks the surrounding documentary ecology that a real presidential authorisation should have. ## What Would Change the Assessment

The Truman authorisation claim would become much stronger if a verifiable record appeared from an official collection with a clean chain of custody. The most valuable evidence would not be another privately circulated scan or a later typed transcript. It would be a record discovered in a relevant archival series, with accession history, file context, classification markings consistent with the era, related routing records, and cross-references in Truman, Forrestal, Bush, CIA, NSC, Air Force or Joint Chiefs holdings.

A convincing record trail would also need to explain the alleged executive-order language. If “Special Classified Executive Order #092447” was not a normal numbered executive order, then what category of presidential instrument was it? Who controlled it? How was it filed? Who received copies? How did it interact with the newly created national security structure? Without answers to those questions, the phrase functions more like dramatic decoration than administrative evidence.

On the present record, the authority claim is the MJ-12 story’s greatest weakness as well as its greatest hook.
