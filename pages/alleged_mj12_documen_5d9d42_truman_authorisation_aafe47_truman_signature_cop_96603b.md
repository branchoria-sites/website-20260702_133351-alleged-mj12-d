## Introduction

One of the most influential objections to the alleged [Truman authorisation]({{'truman-memo/' | relative_url }}) memo concerns not its wording but its signature. Sceptical investigator Philip J. Klass argued that President Harry S. Truman's signature on the purported 24 September 1947 memorandum to Secretary of Defence [James Forrestal]({{'forrestal/' | relative_url }}) was not an independently written signature at all. Instead, he claimed it was a copied photographic reproduction taken from a genuine Truman document dated 1 October 1947. If correct, that finding would indicate that the memo was assembled using material from an authentic presidential record rather than being an original government document.<img src="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_truman_authorisation_aafe47_truman_signature_cop_96603b-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Signature illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_truman_authorisation_aafe47_truman_signature_cop_96603b-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_truman_authorisation_aafe47_truman_signature_cop_96603b-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">
This argument became one of the most frequently cited document-specific criticisms of the MJ-12 papers because it relies on a concrete comparison rather than speculation about motives or conspiracy. Although supporters of the documents have disputed Klass's conclusions, the alleged duplication of the signature remains one of the central forensic challenges to the claimed Truman authorisation.

## What Klass Claimed About the Copied Signature

Philip J. Klass first set out the argument in a series of investigations published in *Skeptical Inquirer* during the late 1980s. He compared the signature appearing on the alleged Truman–Forrestal memorandum with Truman's signature on a genuine White House memorandum sent to Dr [Vannevar Bush]({{'bush/' | relative_url }}) on 1 October 1947.

According to Klass, the two signatures were not merely similar in style. He argued they were photographically identical, including tiny accidental imperfections that should not recur in two independently signed documents. His conclusion was that the MJ-12 signature had been copied from the authentic Bush memorandum and then reproduced on the alleged Forrestal memo.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cdn.centerforinquiry.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/1990/01/22165233/p30.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdn.centerforinquiry.org">[cdn.centerforinquiry.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdn.centerforinquiry.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">September 24, 1947, is suspiciously like the signature on the letter that. Truman wrote to...Read more...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 24, 1947</span></span></span>

The allegation went beyond normal handwriting comparison. Truman, like most public officials, signed thousands of documents with a recognisable style. Similarity alone would therefore prove very little. Klass instead focused on minute physical features that would normally arise only during reproduction of a particular document rather than during the act of signing.

Supporters of the MJ-12 papers rejected this interpretation, arguing that presidential signatures often appear highly consistent and that copying had not been conclusively demonstrated. Nevertheless, Klass's analysis shifted debate away from general handwriting style towards whether the signature represented a unique duplicated image.

## Why Scratch Marks Matter in Document Comparison

The strongest element of Klass's argument involved what he described as accidental scratch marks or blemishes.

When documents are photocopied or photographically reproduced, incidental defects on the source document—such as scratches, fibres, dust, ink interruptions or paper damage—can be transferred along with the signature itself. If the same imperfections appear in precisely the same locations on another supposedly independent document, document examiners generally regard that as evidence that one image originated from the other or from a common photographic source.

Klass argued that the Truman signature on the alleged MJ-12 memo reproduced these incidental marks exactly, including small flaws that had no reason to appear on a separately signed presidential memorandum. He maintained that these shared defects were more persuasive than similarities in pen strokes alone because accidental artefacts are not consciously created by a signer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cdn.centerforinquiry.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/1990/01/22165233/p30.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdn.centerforinquiry.org">[cdn.centerforinquiry.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdn.centerforinquiry.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">September 24, 1947, is suspiciously like the signature on the letter that. Truman wrote to...Read more...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 24, 1947</span></span></span>

In practical terms, the argument is straightforward:<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* Genuine handwritten signatures can naturally resemble one another.
* Accidental scratches and image defects should not repeat identically on separately created originals.
* Identical imperfections are therefore consistent with photographic copying rather than fresh signing.</div>

This reasoning explains why the debate over tiny marks attracted disproportionate attention. The issue was never simply whether Truman's handwriting looked authentic; it was whether the entire signature image appeared to have been mechanically duplicated.<img src="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_truman_authorisation_aafe47_truman_signature_cop_96603b-Illustration-2-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Signature illustration 2" data-theme-src-dark="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_truman_authorisation_aafe47_truman_signature_cop_96603b-Illustration-2-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_truman_authorisation_aafe47_truman_signature_cop_96603b-Illustration-2-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">
## How the Signature Problem Weakens the Wider Briefing Story

Within the MJ-12 narrative, the alleged Truman memorandum serves as the documentary foundation for presidential authorisation of Operation Majestic Twelve. If its signature is copied from another authenticated document, confidence in the memo's originality is substantially reduced.

The significance extends beyond one page. The alleged Truman memo is intended to authenticate the later Eisenhower briefing document by providing a presidential origin for the supposed programme. A copied signature therefore raises the possibility that an authentic historical document was used as raw material in constructing a persuasive forgery.

## Why the Debate Still Matters

The Truman signature controversy remains one of the clearest examples of how a single physical feature can influence assessment of an entire historical document. Unlike arguments about hidden programmes or missing [archives]({{'archives/' | relative_url }}), the copied-signature claim is based on comparative examination of surviving documents.

For critics, the repeated scratch marks provide strong evidence that the alleged authorisation memo incorporated a photographic copy of a genuine Truman signature, making the document highly unlikely to be an original presidential memorandum.

For supporters, the comparison has never been accepted as definitively conclusive, and some continue to argue that the wider MJ-12 case should not stand or fall on one disputed piece of forensic analysis.

Even so, the signature comparison remains one of the most document-specific and frequently cited challenges to the alleged Truman authorisation memo, because if the signature itself was copied, the credibility of the memo as evidence for presidential approval of MJ-12 is significantly undermined.<img src="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_truman_authorisation_aafe47_truman_signature_cop_96603b-Illustration-3-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Signature illustration 3" data-theme-src-dark="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_truman_authorisation_aafe47_truman_signature_cop_96603b-Illustration-3-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_truman_authorisation_aafe47_truman_signature_cop_96603b-Illustration-3-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><section class="further-reading-section" data-page-toc-exclude aria-labelledby="further-reading-title">
