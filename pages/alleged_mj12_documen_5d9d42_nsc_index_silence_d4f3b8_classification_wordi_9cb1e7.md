## Introduction

One of the recurring criticisms of the alleged MJ-12 papers concerns not what they say about UFOs, but how they are marked. Historians of government records often treat classification markings as administrative fingerprints: they reflect the rules, terminology and document conventions of the [bureaucracy]({{'bureaucracy/' | relative_url }}) that produced them. When those markings do not match established practice, they become evidence that a document may not belong to the office or period it claims to represent.<img src="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_nsc_index_silence_d4f3b8_classification_wordi_9cb1e7-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Bad Markings illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_nsc_index_silence_d4f3b8_classification_wordi_9cb1e7-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_nsc_index_silence_d4f3b8_classification_wordi_9cb1e7-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">
In the case of the disputed MJ-12 papers, critics argue that some of the classification wording and handling markings do not fit known [National Security]({{'1947-context/' | relative_url }}) Council (NSC) practice during the Eisenhower administration. While no single anomaly conclusively proves forgery, the classification language forms part of a wider pattern of documentary inconsistencies that archivists and records specialists have identified.

## What the disputed marking says

Several of [the alleged MJ-12 documents]({{'the-alleged-mj-12-documents/' | relative_url }}) bear combinations of classification and handling markings that have attracted sustained scrutiny. Among the most discussed are combinations such as"[Top Secret]({{'top-secret/' | relative_url }})","Restricted", and various compartment-style notations presented together on the same document.

At first glance this may seem unremarkable. Government documents often contain multiple markings. The important question, however, is whether those particular combinations existed within the administrative system the document claims to come from.

Under President Eisenhower's Executive Order 10501, issued in 1953, the United States national security classification system was standardised around only three primary security classifications:<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Top Secret
* Secret<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/Majestic%2012" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[vault.fbi.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Majestic 12</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">12... Majestic-12…” claiming to be highly classified government document. The memo appeared to be a briefing for newly-elected President...</span></span></span>
* Confidential</div>

The order explicitly stated that no other designation should be used to classify defence information unless specifically authorised by statute.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/executive-order-10501-safeguarding-official-information-the-interests-the-defense-the" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: presidency.ucsb.edu">[The American Presidency Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">presidency.ucsb.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The American Presidency ProjectExecutive Order 10501—Safeguarding Official Information...Official information which requires protection...</span></span></span>

## When the reported wording came into NSC use

Critics of the MJ-12 papers argue that some wording appearing on the alleged documents resembles later Cold War classification practices more closely than those of the early 1950s.

For example, some terminology commonly seen on intelligence documents from the 1960s, 1970s and later—such as detailed declassification instructions, paragraph markings and increasingly elaborate compartment systems—was not yet part of routine NSC paperwork in mid-1950s records. Modern guidance from the Information Security Oversight Office illustrates how classification markings evolved over subsequent decades, including features that simply were not standard during the Eisenhower administration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/faqs/marking" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesMarking3 Jan 2025 — In more recent documents, individual paragraphs may also be marked with markings like &quot;(S)&quot; for Secr...</span></span></span>

Researchers therefore examine not merely whether a document is marked"Top Secret", but whether the entire presentation—including typography, placement, accompanying control language and combinations of markings—matches authenticated documents from the same office and period.

This approach is standard archival practice. Document authentication depends on consistency with contemporary administrative procedures rather than on any single phrase viewed in isolation.<div class="youtube-embed-container youtube-embed-fallback"><div class="youtube-embed-card"><div class="youtube-embed-frame"><iframe src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/ogJplVuElLs" title="A secret history: Inside shadow intelligence, the MJ-12 documents | Reality Check" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture; web-share" allowfullscreen></iframe></div><div class="youtube-embed-footer"><p class="youtube-embed-title"><a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ogJplVuElLs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">A secret history: Inside shadow intelligence, the MJ-12 documents | Reality Check</a></p><p class="youtube-embed-meta">Channel: NewsNation &middot; Views: 247.8K &middot; Uploaded: April 2026 &middot; Length: 1 hour 20 minutes</p><p class="youtube-embed-actions"><a class="youtube-embed-watch-link" href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ogJplVuElLs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" title="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ogJplVuElLs">Open on YouTube</a></p></div></div></div>

## Why NSC practice matters more than generic government practice

An important point is that the disputed documents are presented as National Security Council material, not simply as generic military or intelligence papers.

Each major federal organisation maintained its own document-production habits. Even when agencies operated under the same executive order governing classification, they differed in:<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* page layouts;
* routing conventions;
* distribution markings;
* registry references;
* filing practices; and
* internal terminology.</div>

Because of this, historians compare alleged NSC documents with authenticated NSC memoranda from the same period rather than with unrelated classified papers from elsewhere in government.

If a memorandum claims to originate within the NSC system but adopts wording or formatting inconsistent with genuine NSC files, that inconsistency carries greater evidential weight than a mismatch against broader federal practice.<img src="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_nsc_index_silence_d4f3b8_classification_wordi_9cb1e7-Illustration-2-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Bad Markings illustration 2" data-theme-src-dark="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_nsc_index_silence_d4f3b8_classification_wordi_9cb1e7-Illustration-2-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_nsc_index_silence_d4f3b8_classification_wordi_9cb1e7-Illustration-2-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">
## Classification markings as administrative evidence

Classification markings are valuable precisely because they were governed by rules rather than individual preference.

Executive Orders 10290 and later 10501 attempted to standardise how security information was classified, transmitted and protected across executive agencies. Although departments retained some administrative flexibility, the basic language of classification was prescribed rather than improvised.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trumanlibrary.gov/library/executive-orders/10290/executive-order-10290" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trumanlibrary.gov">[Truman Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trumanlibrary.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">executive order 10290</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Truman LibraryEXECUTIVE ORDER 10290 &#124; Harry S. TrumanDocuments and material containing security information which are classified &quot;Top Sec...</span></span></span>

For archivists, this means a questionable marking is not merely a stylistic oddity. It can indicate that:<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a document combines terms from different administrative systems;
* terminology has been borrowed from a later period;
* security markings have been misunderstood by the document's creator; or
* authentic-looking elements have been assembled without accurately reproducing historical bureaucratic practice.</div>

## How the markings fit the wider records problem

The disputed classification wording does not stand alone. It is considered alongside broader archival difficulties surrounding the alleged MJ-12 papers.

The U.S. National [Archives]({{'archives/' | relative_url }}) has reported that extensive searches of National Security Council records, policy-paper indexes and meeting-minute indexes failed to locate supporting references to MJ-12,"Majestic", or related project terminology beyond the disputed documents themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying ObjectsThe National Archives has received numerous inquiries concerning document...</span></span></span>

Taken together, the concerns reinforce one another:<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the documentary trail expected for an NSC project is missing;
* the filing and indexing evidence does not support the claimed project;
* some document formatting and classification language appears inconsistent with known period practice.</div>

Importantly, historians generally avoid treating any single discrepancy as decisive. Instead, authentication relies on the cumulative weight of multiple independent indicators.
