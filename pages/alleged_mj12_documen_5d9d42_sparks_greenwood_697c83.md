## Introduction

Brad Sparks and Barry Greenwood’s critique of MJ-12 matters because it shifts the argument away from “are these impressive-looking papers real?” and towards “how did this story enter UFO culture in the first place?” Their central claim is that the MJ-12 narrative did not emerge from a verified chain of [classified records]({{'secret-trails/' | relative_url }}). Instead, they argue that it grew through a [feedback loop]({{'feedback-loop/' | relative_url }}) involving UFO researchers, Air Force Office of Special Investigations figure Richard Doty, Roswell investigator William L. Moore, and material connected to the earlier “Aquarius” document stories. The most distinctive evidence in their case is the Robert Pratt material: secret recordings, notes and transcripts from 1982 that, in their reading, show key MJ-12 ideas circulating before the famous film canister reached Jaime Shandera in 1984.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/24214339/The_Secret_Pratt_Tapes_and_the_Origins_of_MJ_12_Brad_Sparks_and_Barry_Greenwood_" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Academia(PDF) The Secret Pratt Tapes and the Origins of MJ-12 (Brad Sparks &amp; Barry Greenwood)...</span></span></span><img src="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_sparks_greenwood_697c83-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Sparks Greenwood" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">
## The Core Provenance Argument

Sparks and Greenwood’s critique begins with a basic archival problem: MJ-12 lacks a reliable documentary trail outside the disputed papers themselves. The National Archives reports extensive searches of Air Force, Joint Chiefs of Staff, Truman Library, Eisenhower Library and [National Security]({{'1947-context/' | relative_url }}) Council holdings for terms including MJ-12, Majestic, UFO, flying saucers, extraterrestrial biological entities and [Aquarius]({{'aquarius/' | relative_url }}); those searches were negative apart from the controversial Cutler-Twining memorandum.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

That absence is central to their reasoning. In their 2007 MUFON Symposium paper, Sparks and Greenwood wrote that no indisputably genuine government document has established an MJ-12 committee or designation, no genuine files have shown the activities of such a twelve-member body, and no staff assistants to the alleged committee had surfaced. They also noted that the alleged MJ-12 [members]({{'members/' | relative_url }}) named in the core documents were all conveniently dead before the papers appeared, making direct confirmation impossible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/24214339/The_Secret_Pratt_Tapes_and_the_Origins_of_MJ_12_Brad_Sparks_and_Barry_Greenwood_" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Academia(PDF) The Secret Pratt Tapes and the Origins of MJ-12 (Brad Sparks &amp; Barry Greenwood)...</span></span></span>

Their argument is not merely that the documents contain suspicious formatting or disputed signatures. It is that the story’s route into the UFO community looks wrong for a genuine high-level secret. Instead of appearing through a traceable declassification, archival release or corroborated insider record, MJ-12 appears through anonymous film, hints from “inside sources”, private contacts, withheld postcards, and a cluster of people already involved in Roswell research and intelligence-themed UFO claims. Sparks and Greenwood therefore treat provenance as the decisive weakness: the papers are not just unauthenticated; they appear to have been shaped inside the UFO research circuit that later presented them as external confirmation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/24214339/The_Secret_Pratt_Tapes_and_the_Origins_of_MJ_12_Brad_Sparks_and_Barry_Greenwood_" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Academia(PDF) The Secret Pratt Tapes and the Origins of MJ-12 (Brad Sparks &amp; Barry Greenwood)...</span></span></span>

This is why their critique is more damaging than a simple debunking checklist. A forged document can contain real names, plausible dates and authentic-looking security language. Sparks and Greenwood’s stronger point is that the MJ-12 papers seem to echo what Moore, Stanton Friedman and their circle had already been researching or discussing. In their view, that makes the documents look less like an independent leak and more like a manufactured mirror of the researchers’ own expectations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/24214339/The_Secret_Pratt_Tapes_and_the_Origins_of_MJ_12_Brad_Sparks_and_Barry_Greenwood_" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Academia(PDF) The Secret Pratt Tapes and the Origins of MJ-12 (Brad Sparks &amp; Barry Greenwood)...</span></span></span><img src="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_sparks_greenwood_697c83-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Sparks Greenwood illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_sparks_greenwood_697c83-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_sparks_greenwood_697c83-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">

## The Feedback Loop Problem

The most useful way to understand the Sparks-Greenwood case is as a feedback-loop critique. In this model, UFO researchers shared Roswell theories, candidate names, dates and hypotheses with supposed insiders. Those insiders then returned documents or stories that appeared to confirm the same research. What looked like external validation may have been recycled input.

Sparks and Greenwood place Richard Doty at the centre of this risk. Doty was a Special Agent with the Air Force Office of Special Investigations at Kirtland Air Force Base, and he appears repeatedly in accounts of early-1980s UFO disinformation, especially around Paul Bennewitz and Linda Moulton Howe. A later summary of their argument describes Moore as involved with Doty and says Sparks and Greenwood presented a loop in which Moore and Friedman gave Doty information from their Roswell investigation, after which documents appeared that supported their conclusions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://podcastufo.com/the-mj-12-documents-who-why-what-where-when/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: podcastufo.com">[Podcast UFO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">podcastufo.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Podcast UFOThe MJ-12 Documents: Who, Why, What, Where, When – Podcast UFO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Podcast UFOThe MJ-12 Documents: Who, Why, What, Where, When – Podcast UFO</span></span></span>

Their paper also points to document-comparison claims. Sparks said he compared MJ-12-related material with the Aquarius document, a suspect Kirtland “frequency jamming” report and a genuine FOIA-released AFOSI UFO report by Doty, concluding that typewriter irregularities, typeface and writing style pointed to the same writer or source. That does not by itself prove who forged the MJ-12 papers, but it reinforces the claim that the material belongs in the Doty-Kirtland-Aquarius environment rather than in a clean 1950s presidential archive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/24214339/The_Secret_Pratt_Tapes_and_the_Origins_of_MJ_12_Brad_Sparks_and_Barry_Greenwood_" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Academia(PDF) The Secret Pratt Tapes and the Origins of MJ-12 (Brad Sparks &amp; Barry Greenwood)...</span></span></span>

This is also where Sparks and Greenwood’s critique differs from purely official scepticism. The FBI Vault page says an Air Force investigation determined the MJ-12 document to be fake, and the FBI file is famous for copies stamped “BOGUS”. The National Archives and GAO record searches weigh against [authenticity]({{'authenticity/' | relative_url }}) from the institutional side. Sparks and Greenwood add a social and narrative explanation: they try to show how the claim could have been generated, circulated and believed inside the UFO community before it became a public controversy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/Majestic%2012" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI+2GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">— Federal Bureau of Investigation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FBI — Federal Bureau of Investigation...</span></span></span><img src="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_sparks_greenwood_697c83-Illustration-2-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Sparks Greenwood illustration 2" data-theme-src-dark="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_sparks_greenwood_697c83-Illustration-2-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_sparks_greenwood_697c83-Illustration-2-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">
## Why the Cutler-Twining Memo Does Not Rescue MJ-12

The Cutler-Twining memorandum is the one MJ-12-related item found in National Archives custody, so it naturally became important to defenders of the documents. It is a one-page memorandum dated 14 July 1954 from Robert Cutler to General Nathan Twining, referring to an “NSC/MJ-12 Special Studies Project” briefing on 16 July. The National Archives, however, emphasises that the memo does not identify MJ-12 or the purpose of the briefing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

For Sparks and Greenwood, the Cutler-Twining memo is not a secure anchor but another suspicious object. They note that it is the only MJ-12-related paper with an official-looking archival location, which makes it appear almost tailor-made to lend credibility to the rest of the disputed documents. The National Archives lists multiple problems: the memo lacks the Top Secret register number expected for the file series, no other documents in the folder concern NSC/MJ-12, related searches produced no further information, and the security marking “Top Secret Restricted Information” was not used by the NSC until the Nixon administration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/24214339/The_Secret_Pratt_Tapes_and_the_Origins_of_MJ_12_Brad_Sparks_and_Barry_Greenwood_" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Academia(PDF) The Secret Pratt Tapes and the Origins of MJ-12 (Brad Sparks &amp; Barry Greenwood)...</span></span></span>

The dating problem is especially concrete. National Archives records show that Robert Cutler was visiting military installations overseas on 14 July 1954, the day he supposedly issued the memorandum. The Eisenhower Library also found no appointment-book entry for a special MJ-12 meeting on 16 July 1954, and the National Archives notes that even off-the-record presidential meetings normally left appointment-book traces.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

Sparks and Greenwood therefore treat the Cutler-Twining item as consistent with the wider pattern: a tantalising fragment that looks useful only when isolated, but becomes weaker when placed against normal records, schedules, markings and file context. It does not supply the missing institutional trail for MJ-12; it highlights how thin that trail is.

## Why Their Critique Matters

The Sparks-Greenwood critique matters because it gives readers a way to evaluate MJ-12 without relying only on instinctive belief or disbelief. It asks a practical historical question: did MJ-12 arise from verifiable government records, or from a chain of claims, hints, recycled research and suspect documents?

Their answer is that MJ-12 is best understood as a modern UFO-era construction, not a demonstrated Truman-era committee. That conclusion aligns with official findings that searches failed to locate a supporting record trail and that Air Force investigators regarded the documents as fabricated. But Sparks and Greenwood go further by trying to reconstruct the path by which the story may have been assembled: Aquarius stories, Doty-linked material, Moore’s Roswell research, Pratt’s recordings, Shandera’s anonymous film, and later attempts to use the Cutler-Twining memo as validation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives+2FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

Their case also explains why MJ-12 remained persuasive despite weak provenance. The documents seemed to reward prior research. They named plausible officials, used Cold War secrecy language, placed events at historically meaningful moments, and supplied the kind of hidden structure UFO researchers had long suspected. Sparks and Greenwood’s warning is that those strengths can be signs of targeted fabrication: a good hoax does not look random; it looks like it knows exactly what its intended audience wants to find.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/24214339/The_Secret_Pratt_Tapes_and_the_Origins_of_MJ_12_Brad_Sparks_and_Barry_Greenwood_" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Academia(PDF) The Secret Pratt Tapes and the Origins of MJ-12 (Brad Sparks &amp; Barry Greenwood)...</span></span></span>

Defenders of MJ-12 have challenged Sparks and Greenwood’s conclusions. Robert M. Wood and Ryan S. Wood, for example, argued in a 2007 response that Sparks and Greenwood had not made the Pratt tapes independently available for assessment and that they had described an early hoax but not necessarily the origin of all MJ-12 documents. That dispute matters because the Sparks-Greenwood argument depends partly on interpreting private or semi-private tape material and reconstructing a chain of influence rather than producing a single smoking-gun confession.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://majesticdocuments.com/pdf/The-New-Debunkers-Rehash-Klass.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: majesticdocuments.com">[majesticdocuments.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">majesticdocuments.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Microsoft Word</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Microsoft Word</span></span></span>

Even with that limitation, their critique remains one of the most important MJ-12 origin analyses because it changes the burden of proof. A reader does not need to prove every step of an alleged hoax operation to see the risk. Once the same names, dates and themes can be traced through prior UFO research channels and Doty-linked stories, the documents lose their strongest rhetorical force: the claim that they arrived as independent, classified confirmation from outside the UFO community.<img src="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_sparks_greenwood_697c83-Illustration-3-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Sparks Greenwood illustration 3" data-theme-src-dark="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_sparks_greenwood_697c83-Illustration-3-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{"/assets/images/alleged_mj12_documen_5d9d42_sparks_greenwood_697c83-Illustration-3-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">
## The Takeaway

Sparks and Greenwood’s MJ-12 critique is best read as a provenance argument, not just a debunking exercise. They argue that MJ-12 grew out of a questionable channel in which Roswell research, Aquarius claims, Doty-linked material and anonymous document drops fed into one another. The Pratt tapes matter because they appear to place key parts of the later MJ-12 mythology in circulation before the famous 1984 Shandera film, weakening the idea that the film represented a sudden leak from a genuine 1950s archive.

The result is a sharper way to read [the alleged MJ-12 documents]({{'the-alleged-mj-12-documents/' | relative_url }}). Their problem is not only that official searches failed to authenticate them, or that individual details can be challenged. Their deeper problem is that the origin story looks contaminated: the documents appear to reflect the expectations, theories and insider games of early-1980s UFO culture more clearly than they reflect the record-keeping habits of the Truman and Eisenhower national-security state.
